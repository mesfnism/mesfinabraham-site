Executive Summary

This weekly edition of EthioSkills Intelligence tracked 4016 vacancy postings from twelve of fourteen configured sources, standardized against international occupation, skills, education and industry taxonomies so that figures reported here are comparable across sources and over time. The purpose of this exercise is not to replace Ethiopia's official labour market information architecture, but to complement it. The national E-LMIS and the Ethiopian Statistical Service's household and establishment surveys provide periodic, authoritative but infrequent measurement of the labour market [1][3]. This report offers a continuously updated, narrower read of what employers are actually advertising for, close to real time. It tracks advertised demand. It does not measure labour shortages, unemployment or actual hiring outcomes, which require the survey and administrative sources cited above.

The skill most often written into postings this period was Communication, the leading economic activity by posting count was Professional, scientific and technical activities, and the employer with the most postings under a stated name was Bule Hora University. 557 postings (13.9 percent) name no real employer, because the board shows a placeholder such as Company or Private Client or the poster is a recruiting intermediary. They are not ranked as employers. A posting mentioning a skill is not by itself evidence that the skill is scarce or that employers struggle to find it. Relative to the comparable week window around 2026-10-01, tracked volume shows an increase of 150.5 percent, which this report treats as an indicative signal of market movement in the short run rather than a precise measure of total vacancy creation in the economy. The Analysis section below sets out these patterns in full, with supporting tables and figures. The Data Sources and Limitations sections set out, without qualification, what this exercise can and cannot yet claim to measure.

Introduction

Ethiopia's labour market is widely characterised, in both the academic and policy literature, by high youth unemployment, informality, and a persistent mismatch between the skills job seekers hold and the skills employers require [1][3]. A recurring theme in assessments of the country's labour market information architecture is fragmentation. Multiple institutions, including the Ministry of Labour and Skills, the Ethiopian Statistical Service, the Ethiopian Investment Commission, and programme offices funded by donors, each hold partial data collected administratively, with limited interoperability or routine sharing of data between them [1][2]. Ethiopia's own E-LMIS initiative, evolving through systems including Ethioworks and JEDI, is a direct institutional response to this gap, but remains a work in progress [1][2].

EthioSkills Intelligence takes a narrower, complementary approach. Rather than attempting to measure the labour market as a whole, it tracks what is observable directly and continuously, namely vacancy postings advertised on Ethiopia's most used online job boards. Each posting is read, structured, and classified against four international standards. ESCO for skills, ISCO-08 for occupations, ISCED 2011 for education levels, and ISIC Rev.4 for industry sectors [4][5][6][7]. This makes a posting's requirements comparable across sources and legible to anyone already familiar with those schemes, including statistical agencies and development partners.

This weekly report covers postings first seen from 2026-10-01 to 2026-10-08, within a look-back of up to 7 days. It is one of two companion editions produced on a rolling basis. A weekly edition captures movement over the short run, and a monthly edition captures a steadier baseline. Both are generated automatically from the same underlying dataset and published alongside a live, continuously updated dashboard.

Scope and methodology, briefly

Fourteen sources are configured, and twelve of them contributed postings to this edition. EthioJobs, HaHuJobs, Ethiopian Reporter Jobs, PalmJobs, DevNetJobs (filtered to postings relevant to Ethiopia), HarmeeJobs, ElelanaJobs, KebenaJobs, Afriworket, GeezJobs, NGOJobs, ReliefWeb (the UN's aggregator for humanitarian and development jobs, queried through its own API), and postings found on X through a Grok assisted search. Thirteen of these fourteen are scraped or queried automatically on a daily schedule. The last, X, is added through a manual, occasional search rather than a scheduled call, and is marked as such wherever it appears in this report. A posting that has been cross posted to more than one source is identified and merged rather than double counted, matched on employer, job title and location within a short window around the posting date. Each of the four taxonomies above is applied independently per field. A posting that cannot be confidently classified against a given taxonomy is excluded from that specific breakdown rather than guessed at, and is still counted toward overall volume. The Data Sources section that follows sets out what each source does and does not capture well. The Limitations section sets out what this leaves unresolved.

Collection history matters for how to read the figures. The postings in this window were first seen between 2026-10-01 and 2026-10-08, which is 8 calendar days and 8 scrape days. A window label such as 30 days sets the longest period the report looks back over. It does not mean that 30 days of postings were collected. The first runs of a source also capture every posting already live on that board, so a posting's first seen date is not its posting date.

What this report measures, and what it does not

It measures

  • Online vacancy advertisements from twelve of fourteen configured sources
  • Skills, qualifications and experience as advertised in those postings
  • Employer and economic activity patterns within that advertised set
  • Period to period change in what is advertised online

It does not measure

  • Total vacancies or total labour demand in Ethiopia
  • Unemployment or skill shortages
  • Whether a posting results in an actual hire
  • Informal or unadvertised recruitment
  • Postings on boards that are not collected, including Ethiopian Reporter Jobs

Data Sources

Each source below is described in terms of what it covers, what it is reliably good for, where it currently falls short, and what a realistic next step to improve it looks like. None of the sources is treated as authoritative on its own. The value of combining them is in triangulating signal across boards that would otherwise sit in isolation from each other, in the same spirit recommended for Ethiopia's wider system of labour market information [1].

EthioJobs

Scope. Ethiopia's largest general job board, spanning private companies, banks, manufacturers, schools and some NGOs.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is the largest and steadiest volume of any single source tracked here, with structured fields (career level, education, years of experience, required skills) read directly from each posting's detail page rather than inferred.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that postings rarely state a wage figure, so salary coverage for this source alone is thin, and a posting written only in Amharic is not yet parsed into the skills, education or sector classifiers.

Possible improvement. Extending the skills and category classifiers to Amharic text, and cross-checking a sample of postings against the employer's own site for wage information where available.

In this edition. 371 postings, which is 9.2 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

HaHuJobs

Scope. A second major Ethiopian job board, covering a mix of employers similar to EthioJobs in the private sector, with heavier representation from public institutions and universities.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is being one of the two largest sources by volume, with a heavy share of public institutions and universities that the other boards carry less often.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that collection reads the listing page only, so most postings carry a title, employer, location and deadline but no description, skills list or posting date (Table 8 gives the exact shares). Some postings from public bodies and universities are also written in Amharic, which the current classifiers cannot read. Those postings count toward total volume but not toward the Skills, Education or Sector breakdowns.

Possible improvement. Fetching each posting's detail page for its description and posting date would lift coverage the most, since this source supplies a large share of all rows. Extending language coverage to Amharic would recover the postings the classifiers cannot read.

In this edition. 1620 postings, which is 40.3 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

Ethiopian Reporter Jobs

Scope. The vacancy section of an Ethiopian weekly newspaper that has run for many years. It carries a distinct mix of postings, with a larger share of public sector and NGO notices than the job boards that operate purely online.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is being independent of the online job boards, so it surfaces vacancies, particularly notices from public institutions, that may not be posted anywhere else.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that the site runs bot detection that blocks an automated browser, and repeated attempts to collect it have not succeeded. The count for this edition is shown below. By the 2025 national Labour Market Intelligence report this board carries about a quarter of national online postings [9], so a gap here is a material gap in coverage and is stated in the Limitations section.

Possible improvement. Access has to be arranged with the board or found by another route. Until then its postings are not in the figures of this report.

In this edition. 701 postings, which is 17.5 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

PalmJobs

Scope. A newer Ethiopian job board drawing on a modern web stack, covering a mix of employers broadly similar to EthioJobs and HaHuJobs in the private sector.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is structured data read directly from the site's own API, giving clean fields with little parsing of free text required once the connection to that API is working.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that the site has changed its own technical architecture more than once since tracking began, each time silently breaking the pipeline's connection to it until corrected. It also supplies no education field and states skills for few postings.

Possible improvement. The pipeline reads the site's public API key directly from its code, and the source reliability table shows when a run returns no new postings, so the next change on the site is caught quickly.

In this edition. 591 postings, which is 14.7 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

DevNetJobs

Scope. An international vacancy board for the development sector. This pipeline filters it to postings relevant to Ethiopia only, using a keyword search, so it is deliberately narrower than the general job boards.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is a distinct slice of the market oriented toward international work, donor roles, NGO roles and development programme roles, that general job boards rarely carry.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that resolving a listing into its full detail page depends on replaying the site's own interactive behaviour, which is fragile by nature, and a share of its postings are visible to members only and are correctly skipped rather than guessed at. Both factors keep its tracked volume very low.

Possible improvement. The pipeline drives that interaction through a real browser page rather than approximating it. If volume stays low, the keyword search itself, scoped narrowly to avoid noise from listings visible only to members, is the next thing to widen.

In this edition. 5 postings, which is 0.1 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

HarmeeJobs

Scope. An Ethiopian job board read via its own public RSS feed rather than by parsing rendered HTML.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is the cleanest and most stable connection of any source tracked here, since an RSS feed is a stable, structured contract that a site redesign is unlikely to break.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that an RSS feed typically carries less structured detail per posting than a full detail page, so some fields (years of experience, a specific salary figure) are stated less often here than for sources read from full detail pages.

Possible improvement. Where the feed links back to a full posting page, fetching that page for the subset of fields it still lacks is a natural next step.

In this edition. 81 postings, which is 2.0 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

ElelanaJobs

Scope. A smaller Ethiopian job board read via its own public RSS feed, carrying a mix of banking, insurance and private sector postings.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is the full posting text comes through in the feed itself, with no separate page fetch needed, the same stable connection advantage HarmeeJobs has.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that many of its postings are bulletins announcing several distinct roles at one employer under a single headline rather than one posting per role, so this report counts each bulletin once rather than once per role within it, and fields such as education and years of experience, which would need to be attributed to a specific role within a multi role bulletin, are left unstated rather than guessed at.

Possible improvement. Splitting a multi role bulletin into one tracked posting per numbered role is the natural next step, and would also make education and experience extractable per role instead of left blank.

In this edition. 18 postings, which is 0.4 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

KebenaJobs

Scope. A smaller Ethiopian job board, built on the same underlying software as ElelanaJobs and read the same way, via its own public RSS feed.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is the same stable, full text RSS connection as ElelanaJobs.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that the same multi role bulletin pattern described for ElelanaJobs above.

Possible improvement. The same fix described for ElelanaJobs above would apply here too.

In this edition. No postings from this source are in this edition's data.

Afriworket

Scope. A modern Ethiopian job platform covering a broad mix of private sector roles, including a notable share of informal, freelance and gig style postings that the older general job boards rarely carry.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is the richest structured detail page of any source tracked here, stating economic activity, education qualification, vacancy count, experience level and a defined skill list as separate fields rather than mixed into free text.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that a posting's pay is disclosed as a specific monthly figure only for some roles, a generic pay period label appears even when no figure is given, and this report keeps only the figure, treating the label alone as not stating a salary, so salary coverage for this source reflects what employers actually disclosed rather than an artifact of the page's own layout.

Possible improvement. No fix is currently planned. The gap is a property of what employers choose to disclose on this platform, not a parsing limitation.

In this edition. 511 postings, which is 12.7 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

GeezJobs

Scope. A general Ethiopian job board covering a broad mix of private sector, NGO and public roles.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is a detail page that states work type, contract type, years of experience and a specific deadline date as separate fields, and in many postings an education requirement embedded in the description text that this pipeline extracts directly.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that the site exposes only its own front page of featured and latest postings, roughly seventy at a time, with no further pagination or search API behind it, so this source's tracked history is bounded by what the site itself chooses to keep visible rather than by anything this pipeline could walk further into.

Possible improvement. None is available from this pipeline alone. A deeper archive would require the site itself to expose one.

In this edition. 120 postings, which is 3.0 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

NGOJobs

Scope. A discovery site for vacancies at NGOs, UN agencies, foundations and development organisations working in Ethiopia. It does not employ anyone and sends each applicant to the employer's own application page.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is every posting carries structured data with an exact posting date, an exact closing date, the named hiring organisation, the town and the full description, so dates and employers need no estimating, and it lists many national staff roles at international NGOs that other sources cover less fully.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that it states no career level, education level, skills or salary as separate fields, so those are read from the description text only where an employer wrote them down, and a posting can also appear on ReliefWeb or DevNetJobs, where this report merges it.

Possible improvement. None is needed from the site. Reading education and experience from the description more fully is the natural next step.

In this edition. 203 postings, which is 5.1 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

X (Grok search)

Scope. Job postings announced directly on X by employers, recruiters and job board accounts, found through a Grok search rather than scraped from a job board's own pages. This is the only tracked source that reaches postings made entirely on social media, with no job board listing behind them at all, and it is where United Nations agencies, embassies and international NGOs are most often found in this dataset.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is it surfaces employers that do not advertise on any of the job boards tracked here, mainly international organizations, embassies and NGOs that announce a vacancy directly from their own account rather than through a local job board.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that most postings from this source state no work type, employment type, career level, required skills, education level or salary figure, since a tweet rarely spells those out the way a job board's structured detail page does, and no category tag exists for a tweet at all, so this source adds employer and role coverage far more than it adds classified detail. It is also collected through an occasional manual search rather than a daily automated scrape, so its volume reflects when a search was last run, not a complete or current picture of what is on X at any given moment.

Possible improvement. Running the search on a regular cadence rather than once, and splitting a single broad search into several narrower ones by actor type, such as a search aimed specifically at banks and financial institutions, since a single broad search tends to surface only a partial slice of what is actually posted. For the UN agency, embassy and NGO segment specifically, ReliefWeb below is now a better targeted source than searching X for the same postings.

In this edition. No postings from this source are in this edition's data.

ReliefWeb

Scope. The United Nations' own aggregator for humanitarian and development sector jobs, queried through its public API rather than scraped from rendered pages, filtered here to postings tagged with Ethiopia as the country.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is structured fields read directly from the API rather than inferred from free text, including the hiring organization, career category, years of experience required, posting and closing dates, and the full job description, covering exactly the UN agency, embassy and international NGO segment that Ethiopia's own job boards rarely carry.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that the API has no dedicated skills or salary field at all, consistent with this sector's own low rate of pay disclosure noted elsewhere in this report, so those two fields are structurally absent for every posting from this source, not just thin. Education level is only available when a posting happens to state it as a line inside its own description text.

Possible improvement. No fix changes the missing skills or salary fields, since the API simply does not carry them. Education coverage could improve with a more thorough pass at the varied ways a description states its requirement, beyond the single line format currently recognized.

In this edition. 21 postings, which is 0.5 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

Google Jobs

Scope. The job listings Google gathers from many sites at once, read through a search service and limited to searches about Ethiopia and its main towns.

Strength. Its principal advantage in this dataset is reaching employers and sites that no direct collector here covers, such as LinkedIn Ethiopia, international organisations' own career pages and smaller local boards, and for giving the posting age and the job type for most results.

Weakness. Its main limitation is that no application deadline, skills list or years of experience, and the category is inferred from the job title. The number of searches is capped by the service plan, so each query returns only its first pages of results and the source is a sample rather than a full count of postings.

Possible improvement. Widening the list of searches within the plan, and using this source mainly to find postings and employers that the direct collectors miss.

In this edition. 57 postings, which is 1.4 percent of all postings in the window (a posting carried by two boards counts for both).

Separately from coverage at the source level, this period's data also shows thin coverage on fields that depend on a posting stating them explicitly. Fewer than 50 percent of postings report stating a clear education level, stating a clear monthly pay figure. This is a property of what employers choose to disclose, not a parsing failure, and it is treated as a stated limitation throughout rather than estimated or imputed.

Education, experience and pay profile

Of 4016 postings this period, 1720 (42.8%) state a clear education requirement, 2938 (73.2%) state years of experience required, and only 96 (2.4%) state a clear monthly pay figure. This is consistent with the broader observation that Ethiopian employers disclose compensation far less often than qualifications.

Concentration, Coverage and Source Reliability

This section reports what the rest of the Analysis section does not. It covers how concentrated advertised demand is among a small set of skills, employers or economic activities, how coverage breaks down by employer identity, region and language, and how reliably each tracked source is actually producing new postings. None of this changes what the headline tables say. It is context for how much weight to put on them.

Concentration

  • The top 10 skills account for 64.9 percent of all mentions this period, across 662 distinct entries seen (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index 773.2).
  • The top 10 employers account for 9.8 percent of all mentions this period, across 1185 distinct entries seen (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index 27.0).
  • The top 10 economic activities account for 93.1 percent of all mentions this period, across 13 distinct entries seen (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index 1287.5).

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index runs from 0 to 10,000, and a higher value means mentions sit in fewer entries. There is no accepted benchmark for what counts as high for skills, employers or economic activities in a job advertisement set, so the figure is best used to compare one edition with the next and not to label a single edition as low or high.

Positions, Entry Barriers, Skill Types and Geography

How this report is organised

The report works on three layers. The monthly pulse describes what was advertised in the window. The structural layer describes what persists over six months or more. The graduate to work layer, which would set advertised demand against graduate output and tracer study evidence, is an annual product that depends on supply side data this report does not hold. The structural layer, which would track persistence, emerging occupations and changes in experience requirements, is withheld until at least 180 days of history exist. This edition holds 8 days, so it reports levels and not trends.

Postings and positions

A posting is one advertisement and a position is one vacancy it announces. One posting can announce several positions, so counting postings understates the number of vacancies. 776 postings state two or more positions, and the average among postings that state a number is 2.21. Positions are an estimate because 1138 postings do not state a number.

Advantages, Limitations and Planned Improvements

Advantages of this approach

  • Continuous, low cost measurement that refreshes automatically, in contrast to periodic labour market information based on surveys that updates only every few years.
  • Standardized against the same international taxonomies (ESCO, ISCO-08, ISCED 2011, ISIC Rev.4) used in official reporting on labour markets, so figures here are directly comparable to other work using the same schemes.
  • Full transparency on what is and is not captured. A field that cannot be classified confidently is excluded from that breakdown rather than estimated, and every honesty threshold used in this report (for example a minimum posting count before a skill is reported as most requested for a group) is stated rather than hidden.

Limitations

  • Coverage is restricted to vacancies advertised on the specific online boards tracked. Informal, word of mouth, and unadvertised hiring, a substantial share of actual hiring activity in Ethiopia, is not observed at all.
  • Classification currently works on text written in English. Postings written in Ethiopic script (Amharic or Tigrinya), measured in the language table in the next section, are not yet classified into the Skills, Education or Sector breakdowns, though they are still counted toward overall volume.
  • Region is inferred from a keyword lookup against each posting's stated location, not a verified gazetteer, and a location this lookup does not recognize is left unclassified rather than guessed.
  • Employer type (Private, Public or NGO) is inferred from a keyword heuristic on the employer's own name, not a verified registry lookup, and should be read as indicative.
  • Tracked history is still short relative to the seasonal cycles a labour market can exhibit over a full year. Early trend comparisons should be read cautiously until more history accumulates.

In this edition specifically, coverage is thin for stating a clear education level (42.8% of postings), stating a clear monthly pay figure (2.4% of postings). No postings from KebenaJobs, X (Grok search) are in this edition's data. A missing source is a gap in coverage, and its absence is not evidence that it lists nothing. DevNetJobs, ElelanaJobs, ReliefWeb, Google Jobs each contribute under two percent of postings. X postings, gathered through an occasional manual Grok search and not a scheduled call, are smaller in volume and mostly lack a stated skill, education or salary field, so they add employer and role coverage more than classified detail. They are excluded from the source reliability table, since its stalled run check assumes a daily schedule.

Planned improvements

The priorities for the near future are extending the language coverage of the skills and sector classifiers to Amharic text, closing the volume gap on the three sources that currently fall short, and adding additional sources, including, subject to feasibility and permission, sources beyond Ethiopia's borders within the Horn of Africa. Readers who hold data, funding, or institutional mandate relevant to any of these priorities are invited to make contact (see the cover page for details).

Conclusion

Over this week window, Ethiopia's tracked online vacancy market concentrated around a recognisable core of mentioned skills and economic activity, led by Communication as the skill most often written into postings and Professional, scientific and technical activities as the economic activity with the most postings. This concentration, together with the recurrence observed across reporting periods for a stable set of top mentioned skills and economic activities, is consistent with a set of advertised requirements that holds steady from one period to the next rather than shifting unpredictably. That pattern describes what is advertised, not a confirmed shortage, and it still has implications for how training providers and jobseekers might prioritise their effort.

At the same time, the gaps documented in this report, thin wage disclosure, uneven coverage across sources, and a language barrier on postings written only in Amharic, mean that this report's picture of the market, while directionally useful, remains partial. It is offered as a complement to, not a substitute for, Ethiopia's official labour market information systems, and is intended to improve in coverage and depth with each future edition.

Recommendations

For training providers and TVET institutions

Align curriculum updates with the skills and sectors shown as persistent in this report's Analysis section, since persistence across reporting periods is a stronger signal of durable demand than a single period's figures alone. Where a specific sector's leading skill (Table 2) differs from what a programme currently emphasises, that gap is worth investigating directly with employers in that sector.

For policymakers and labour market information stakeholders

This report's granularity at the sector and skill level, refreshed continuously, is offered as a complementary input to the periodic measurement based on surveys that Ethiopia's official labour market information architecture already produces, and as a concrete example of the kind of administrative data integration recommended in prior assessments of that architecture [1]. Collaboration on sharing data, validation against official sources, or integration into E-LMIS reporting is welcomed.

For jobseekers

The Top Skills table and the tables of leading skill by sector in this report (Analysis section) are a practical starting point for prioritising which skills to acquire or present, particularly where a skill appears as both persistent and leading in its sector rather than a single spike.

Scaling and collaboration

The underlying pipeline is built to extend to further sources, further geographies within the Horn of Africa, and partnership with institutional initiatives in labour market information or ones funded by donors. Readers interested in collaboration, sharing data, or funding this work are invited to make contact (see the cover page for details).
